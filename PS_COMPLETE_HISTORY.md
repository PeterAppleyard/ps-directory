## 1. Company Origins

### The Founding (1961)

**Founders:**
- **Brian Pettit** - Accountant with business acumen
- **Ron Sevitt** - Dublin-born salesman with vision

Both men were executives at **Sun-Line Homes**, a project home company that went into liquidation in 1961. When Sun-Line collapsed, Pettit and Sevitt saw opportunity. Their initial brief was pragmatic: oversee completion of houses Sun-Line had taken deposits for, use those profits to establish their own operation.

**The Mission:**
Make architect-designed homes accessible and affordable for young professionals and middle-class families - people who appreciated good design but couldn't afford to engage an architect directly.

### First Steps (1961-1962)

**Commissioning the Architects:**
Pettit & Sevitt's first move was brilliant: they commissioned two young architects working for the NSW State Government - **Ken Woolley** and **Michael Dysart**.

The pair had previously won attention with a design for the Lend Lease "Parade of Homes" at the Kingsdene estate in Carlingford. Additionally, in 1958, Woolley and Dysart had won Australia's Family Home Competition for a low-cost house design. This became the foundation for P&S's approach.

**The First Houses:**
- **Early 1962**: First Split Level built at **Clifton Gardens, Sydney**
- **Soon after**: Second Split Level at **Richard Road, St Ives**

These two pioneering houses generated enough business and buzz to finance their first exhibition centre. The public response proved that Australians were hungry for well-designed modern homes.

## 2. The Architects

Pettit & Sevitt distinguished itself by working with Australia's leading modernist architects - a roster that reads like a who's-who of mid-century Australian design:

### Ken Woolley (Primary Designer, 1961-1975)

**The Visionary Behind P&S**

Ken Woolley (1933-2015) was the creative force behind Pettit & Sevitt for 15 years and designed over **3,500 P&S homes** - making him arguably Australia's most prolific residential architect.

**Early Life & Education:**
- Born May 29, 1933, grew up in a California Bungalow in Kingsford, NSW
- Showed early talent for art and music (won honors at Conservatorium of Music, age 10)
- **Sydney Boys High School** (disappointed it had no art classes)
- **University of Sydney, School of Architecture**
  - Worked as trainee at NSW Public Works Department (paid his tuition + living expenses)
  - Graduated 1955 with **First Class Honours**
  - Won **University Medal**
  - Won **Byera Hadley Travelling Scholarship** (funded overseas travel and study)
- Traveled USA, Europe, UK (1955-1956), studied emerging modernist trends
- Briefly worked with **Chamberlin, Powell and Bon** in London (1956-57)

**Career at Government Architect's Office (1955-1963):**
- Joined Harry Rembert's Design Room at Government Architect's Branch
- Rose to position of Design Architect
- Designed major public buildings while moonlighting on private residential work

**Major Works Beyond P&S:**

*Government Architect's Office Period:*
- **St Margaret's Chapel and Sisters' Home**, Surry Hills (1955) - Designed age 22, his first autonomous building
- **Fisher Library, University of Sydney** (with T.E. O'Mahony, completed 1963)
  - Won **RAIA Sulman Medal**
  - Won **RIBA Bronze Medal**
  - Undergraduate wing acclaimed as masterpiece
- **State Office Block, Macquarie Street** (later controversially demolished 1998)

*With Ancher Mortlock Murray & Woolley (joined 1964, partner from 1976):*
The practice received every major architectural award and created outstanding buildings including:

- **Australian Embassy, Bangkok** - Internationally acclaimed
- **ABC Radio and Orchestra Centre, Ultimo** - Major cultural infrastructure
- **Park Hyatt Sydney, Campbell's Cove** - Luxury hotel respecting historic context
- **State Library of Victoria** extension - Won 2006 Lachlan Macquarie Award for Heritage Architecture
- **Sydney Airport Control Tower** - Iconic infrastructure
- **RAS Dome and Exhibition Hall**, Homebush - Engineering achievement
- **Olympic Hockey Stadium**, Homebush - Built for Sydney 2000
- **Sydney Convention Centre, Darling Harbour** - Major civic project
- **Town Hall House, Sydney** - Office tower with public square
- **Queen Victoria Building** refurbishment (2009) - Sensitive heritage work
- **Garvan Institute of Medical Research**, Darlinghurst - Features iconic DNA-inspired helical staircase
- **Guided Missile Launching System Repair Facility**, Garden Island - High-security naval project
- **Newcastle University Union Building** - Brutalist concrete masterwork
- **Macquarie University Union Building** - Brutalist concrete
- **The Penthouses, Darling Point** (1967) - Multi-residential innovation

**Personal Houses (All Won Wilkinson Awards):**

Remarkably, Woolley designed three homes for himself over three decades - each radically different from the previous, and **each won the Wilkinson Award** (the only architect ever to achieve this):

1. **Woolley House, Mosman** (1962)
   - Timber and brick Sydney School classic
   - Built down a steep bush-covered slope
   - Based on grid of 12-foot square units staggered by 1/3
   - Natural materials: dark tiles, western red cedar, painted bricks
   - Landscape by Bruce Mackenzie
   - **Won 1962 Wilkinson Award**
   - **NSW State Heritage Listed** (2001)
   - **2022 National Award for Enduring Architecture** (60 years later)
   - Bequeathed to University of NSW (2016)
   - Built by Pettit, Sevitt and Partners

2. **Paddington House** (1983)
   - Arresting white brick modernist structure
   - Signature three-story circular tower (shaped like grand piano heel as homage to his wife's musical ambitions)
   - Urban infill on tight site
   - **Won 1983 Wilkinson Award**

3. **Palm Beach Weekender** (1987)
   - Timber holiday house hugging a cliff
   - Quintessential Sydney beach house
   - **Won 1987 Wilkinson Award**
   - **Won Robin Boyd Award**

**Awards & Honors:**
- **4× Wilkinson Award Winner** (the only architect to achieve this - tied with Harry Seidler for most wins):
  - 1962: Woolley House, Mosman
  - 1968: The Penthouses, Rushcutters Bay
  - 1983: Paddington House
  - 1987: Palm Beach Weekender
- **RAIA Gold Medal** (1993) - Australia's highest architectural honor
- **Sulman Medal** (1962, for Fisher Library)
- **RAIA Blacket Award** (1967, for Newcastle University Union)
- **RAIA Project House Design Awards** (1967, 1968, multiple Western Australia awards)
- **Member of the Order of Australia** (1988)
- **2022 National Award for Enduring Architecture** (posthumous, for Mosman House)
- **Honorary Doctorate** in Architecture
- Fellow of the Australian Academy of Technological Sciences and Engineering (FTSE)
- Over 50 major awards throughout career

**Design Philosophy:**

"The intention is complexity within an ordered discipline." - Ken Woolley

"The large grid and its section set up a rhythm like a beat in music, around which a composition of infill elements is composed." - On the 12-foot module system

He described his work as "late modernist" influenced by:
- **Alvar Aalto** (Finnish architect) - Humanism in modernism
- **Le Corbusier** - Spatial innovation
- **Frank Lloyd Wright** - Organic architecture, integration with landscape

**Teaching & Writing:**
- Visiting professor at Universities of Sydney and New South Wales
- Served on numerous awards and competition juries
- Author of multiple books on architecture
- Was writing theoretical book at time of death

**Prolific Output:**
- Designed over **6,000 houses** (including all P&S work)
- Dozens of major public buildings
- Projects span residential, educational, cultural, sports, commercial, and spiritual architecture
- Nearly all work within 40km of Sydney Town Hall
- Career spanning 60 years

**Legacy:**
Described posthumously as "the most complete architect of the modern era in Australia" - designing "buildings of every type and scale, from small homes and project houses to office towers, apartments, churches, corporate headquarters and civic squares, for public and private clients from prestigious to poor."

**Vale:** November 25, 2015 (age 82)

### Harry Seidler AC OBE (1923-2006)

**Australia's Bauhaus Master**

Harry Seidler brought Bauhaus principles directly from the European masters to Australia and became the first architect to fully express these ideals on Australian soil. He designed select homes for Pettit & Sevitt in the 1960s, lending his international modernist credibility to the brand.

**Background & Education:**
- Born Vienna, Austria, June 25, 1923
- Family fled Nazi occupation (1938-39); clothing factory confiscated
- Classified "enemy alien" in Britain during WWII, sent to Canada (1940)
- **University of Manitoba** - Bachelor of Architecture (1944)
- **Harvard Graduate School of Design** - Studied under **Walter Gropius** and **Marcel Breuer** (founding Bauhaus masters)
- **Black Mountain College, North Carolina** - Studied with painter **Joseph Albers**
- Worked with **Alvar Aalto** in Massachusetts
- Worked with **Marcel Breuer** in New York (late 1946-March 1948)
- Worked with **Oscar Niemeyer** in Rio de Janeiro (April-June 1948)

**Arrival in Australia:**
- June 20, 1948 (shortly before 25th birthday)
- Came only to design house for his parents
- Had no intention to stay
- The house's success convinced him to remain

**Major Works:**

*Residential:*
- **Rose Seidler House, Wahroonga** (1950)
  - First completely modern Bauhaus residence in Australia
  - Caused a sensation
  - **Won Sulman Medal 1951**
  - Now museum (HHT)
- **Seidler House, Killara** (1967)
  - His own family home (co-designed with wife Penelope)
  - **Won Wilkinson Award 1967**
- **Thredbo Ski Lodge** (1965)
  - Multi-level timber building over mountain creek
  - **Won Wilkinson Award 1965**
- **Muller House, Port Hacking** (1966)
  - **Won Wilkinson Award 1966**
- **Horizon Apartments, Darlinghurst** (1999)
  - White curvilinear concrete signature style
  - **Won Wilkinson Award 1999**
- **Basser House, Cammeray** (1959) - House "almost in mid-air" on steep slope over Middle Harbour
- **Hannes House, Cammeray** (1984) - Early expression of curvilinear white concrete form
- **Ithaca Gardens, Elizabeth Bay** (1960)
- **Blues Point Tower, McMahon's Point** (1961) - Controversial harbourside high-rise
- **The Penthouses, Darling Point** (1967)

*Commercial & Public Buildings:*
- **Australia Square** (1961-1967) - Redefined Sydney skyline, first major building to contribute public space back to city
- **MLC Centre** (1972-1978) - Major CBD tower
- **Grosvenor Place** (1982-1988) - CBD tower with public plaza
- **Seidler Office Building and Penthouses, Milsons Point** (1988, 1994) - His own office, curvilinear design
- **Australian Embassy, Paris** (1973-1977) - Critically acclaimed, overlooks Eiffel Tower
- **Ian Thorpe Aquatic Centre, Sydney** - Completed posthumously

*All major CBD projects won Sulman Awards*

**Design Philosophy:**
- Pure Bauhaus principles: marriage of form and function
- Sleek lines, geometric precision
- Harmonious integration with environment
- High-density urban living advocate
- Challenged Sydney's suburban tradition
- Used prestressed concrete innovatively
- Evolved from angular designs to signature white curvilinear forms

**Awards (Selected):**
- **5× Sulman Medals** (more than any other architect)
- **4× Wilkinson Awards** (tied with Ken Woolley for most wins)
- **RAIA Gold Medal** (1976)
- **Royal Institute of British Architects Gold Medal** (1996) - One of architecture's highest international honors
- **Companion of the Order of Australia** (1987)
- **Austria's Gold Cross of Honour (First Class) for Arts and Science** (1996)
- **First Australian member** of Académie d'architecture, Paris (1984)

**Personal Life:**
- Married **Penelope Evatt** (daughter of Clive Evatt), December 15, 1958
- Penelope: architect herself, joined practice 1964 as architect and financial manager
- Two children
- Became Australian citizen 1958 (originally Canadian citizen)

**Controversy & Influence:**
A controversial but towering figure who:
- Regularly criticized planning authorities publicly
- Led 1966 protests to keep Jørn Utzon as Sydney Opera House architect
- Championed modern architecture against conservative resistance
- Changed the face of Sydney over 50 years
- Designed 119 buildings (96 in NSW)
- Consistently won awards across residential, commercial, and public categories for almost 58 years

**Vale:** March 9, 2006

### Michael Dysart OAM

**The Co-Founder of P&S Style**

Michael Dysart co-designed the first P&S houses with Ken Woolley and was instrumental in establishing the company's initial design direction.

**Key Contributions:**
- **1958**: Won **Australia's Family Home Competition** with Ken Woolley for a low-cost house design
- **1961**: Co-designed first P&S models with Woolley for Lend Lease "Parade of Homes" at Kingsdene/Carlingford exhibition
- **Split Level** - Co-designed this foundational P&S model
- **Urambi Village, Canberra** - Significant and historically important medium-density Sydney School housing project

Dysart brought expertise in affordable, well-designed housing and helped establish P&S's modular system approach.

**Current Status:**
Still living as of 2025 and being interviewed for the upcoming Piper Press book documenting complete P&S history (publication expected 2025).

### Russell Jack

**Sydney School Master**

Russell Jack of the renowned practice **Allen, Jack and Cottier** was one of the leading Sydney School architects who brought refined craftsmanship and Japanese-influenced design to P&S.

**Philosophy:**
Influenced by Frank Lloyd Wright's organic architecture and traditional Japanese architecture's "truthful expression of materials and construction aligned with spatial fluidity and flexibility."

**Major Works:**
- **Cater House, Canberra** - Described as "the most highly regarded detached Sydney School house in Canberra"
- **Jack House, Wahroonga** (1956)
  - Exemplifies Sydney School's focus on handcrafted detail
  - Built on a budget with many handmade elements
  - Steel-bracketed bookshelves
  - Walls enclosing terraces to create additional rooms
  - Hides from street behind wild garden and bagged brick wall

**Design Characteristics:**
- Handcrafted details throughout
- Integration of inside/outside spaces
- Use of natural materials in honest expression
- Budget-conscious without compromising design quality

### Neil Clerehan

**Melbourne's Mid-Century Modern Expert**

Neil Clerehan brought Melbourne's refined modernist sensibility to select P&S designs, particularly houses built in Victoria and other southern markets where P&S expanded.

Known for his mastery of Melbourne's mid-century modern residential architecture and his ability to create warm, liveable modernist spaces.

### Bruce Mackenzie

**The Landscape Architect Who Shaped Sydney School Gardens**

Bruce Mackenzie was a pioneer of what became known as the Sydney School in landscape architecture - seeking to enhance Sydney's native bush rather than replace it.

**Philosophy: "Natural Materialism"**
- Enhance Sydney's bush, bring it into gardens
- Bolster native vegetation in new developments
- Use materials to show their true, unaltered nature
- Create settings where houses appear to nestle into landscape

**Major Works:**

*Private Commissions (Rare):*
- **Woolley House, Mosman** (1962)
  - Stone retaining walls laid by hand
  - Original native plantings
  - Rare example of his private garden work

*Public Projects (Better Known For):*
- **UTS Lindfield / Ku-Ring-Gai campus** (former Lindfield TAFE)
- **Botany Bay Foreshore Reserve**
- Many other large-scale public landscape projects

Examples of McKenzie's private garden work are extremely rare - he's primarily known for large public commissions where his bush-regeneration philosophy could operate at scale.

**Legacy:**
His work with P&S display homes helped establish the expectation that modern houses should nestle into, not dominate, their bushland settings.

### The Revolutionary Client Experience

Unlike other project builders of the era, Pettit & Sevitt offered something virtually unheard of: **direct personal contact between client and architect**.

**The Process:**
1. **Initial Consultation** - Buyer meets with the actual architect (Woolley, Seidler, Jack, etc.)
2. **Site Discussion** - Architect advises on best placement for slope, sun, views
3. **Customization Interview** - Client chooses:
   - Interior fit-outs and finishes
   - Kitchen and bathroom configurations
   - Built-in furniture options
   - Window placements
   - Modifications to standard plan
4. **Follow-up** - Architect available for questions during construction

**Why This Mattered:**
- Gave middle-class buyers access to architectural talent usually reserved for wealthy
- Each house felt custom even within modular system
- Buyers understood and appreciated the design principles
- Created educated clients who maintained houses sympathetically
- Distinguished P&S from competitors selling "project homes"

This personal touch was central to P&S's marketing: you weren't just buying a house, you were working with Australia's leading architects to create your ideal modern home.

## 3. Display Centres (Exhibition Villages)

The P&S exhibition centres became cultural phenomena - weekend destinations for Sydney families exploring modern living.

### First Exhibition Centre - Carlingford (1963)

**Location:** Corner of Pennant Hills Road and North Rocks Road, Carlingford

**Background:**
Following success of two pilot houses (Clifton Gardens and St Ives), P&S commissioned a small display village at the Kingsdene estate where Woolley and Dysart had previously exhibited.

**Models Shown:**
- Updated version of the **Split Level**
- The new **Lowline** design (first public showing)

**Public Response:**
Overwhelming. Families flocked to see architect-designed modern homes they could actually afford. The success financed expansion to a much larger exhibition centre.

**Significance:**
Proved the market existed for well-designed project homes. Validated P&S's business model.

---

### Second Exhibition Centre - St Ives (1964)

**Location:** Richmond Road (also referenced as Richmond Avenue), St Ives, Sydney

**Scale:**
This became P&S's flagship display village and a major weekend attraction for Sydney families.

**Models on Display (1964):**

*Single-Level Models:*
- **Lowline** - The most popular model
- **Courtyard**
- **Gallery**
- **Gambrel**

*Split-Level Models:*
- **Split Level Mk 1**
- **Split Level Mk 2**

**The Experience:**
- Fully furnished walk-through homes
- Professional staging showing modern lifestyle
- Landscaping by Bruce Mackenzie demonstrating bush-garden integration
- Sales consultants who understood the architecture
- Opportunity to meet with architects

**Cultural Phenomenon:**
The St Ives display village became a weekend destination:
- Families would visit to experience "modern living"
- See latest kitchen designs and built-in furniture
- Imagine their future homes
- Compare models and customize choices
- Children played while parents toured

**Educational Impact:**
- **1964 Architectural Students Tour** - Photos exist showing Sydney architecture students touring the display homes as part of their education
- Demonstrates how P&S became teaching examples for the next generation of architects

### Belrose Exhibition Centre (Late 1960s)

**Location:** Belrose, Sydney (northern suburbs)

As new subdivisions opened in Sydney's expanding northern suburbs, P&S would establish temporary or permanent display villages to serve those markets.

### Canberra Expansion (Mid-1960s)

While not a traditional display centre, P&S established strong presence in Canberra's developing suburbs:
- Built approximately **500 homes** in Canberra (1966-1978)
- Suburbs included: Pearce, Fisher, Chapman, Aranda
- Canberra's sloping bushland sites were perfect fit for Split Level model

---

### The Display Village Phenomenon

**Why They Mattered:**

1. **Cultural Impact** - Made modern architecture accessible and exciting to average Australians
2. **Weekend Entertainment** - Pre-internet era, visiting display villages was family outing
3. **Education** - Taught people about good design, modern materials, efficient layouts
4. **Aspiration** - Showed what was possible within middle-class budget
5. **Marketing** - Walking through actual houses far more effective than brochures

**Legacy:**
P&S display villages set the template for how project homes would be marketed in Australia for decades to come. The practice of elaborate display villages continues today, though few match the architectural quality P&S offered.

## 4. House Models & Design System

### The Sydney School Foundation

P&S homes exemplified the **"Sydney School"** of architecture (1950s-1980s), a regionalist modernist movement characterized by:

**Design Principles:**
- **Site-responsive** - Buildings follow land contours
- **Landscape integration** - Houses nestle into bush settings
- **Split-level planning** - Multi-levels responding to slopes
- **Natural materials** - Honest expression of brick, timber, stone
- **Indoor-outdoor connection** - Blurred boundaries
- **Climate-appropriate** - Passive solar, natural ventilation

---

### Signature House Models

#### **The Split Level** (1962)

**Designer:** Ken Woolley & Michael Dysart

**The Innovation:**
Designed to work economically on Sydney's characteristically uneven, sloping, rocky terrain - particularly the North Shore's steep bush blocks.

**Design Features:**
- Multi-level design following site contours
- Eliminates need for expensive cut-and-fill earthworks
- Each level offset by half-story
- Creates distinct zones: living, sleeping, service
- Roofs parallel to slope
- Exposed structural beams
- Large windows on living levels
- Decks and terraces at multiple levels

**Why It Succeeded:**
- **Economic** - Worked with difficult sites other builders avoided
- **Spatial** - Levels created interest and defined spaces
- **Views** - Multi-levels maximized vistas
- **Flexible** - Could adapt to various slope angles
- **Sydney-specific** - Solved uniquely local problem

**Impact:**
<cite index="8-1">On the world scene it was an important building because it was an expression of some of the principles of modern architecture and housing. In the hilly terrain of Sydney, the idea was commonly adopted for subsequent housing schemes.</cite>

Became template for how to build on Sydney's challenging topography.

---

#### **The Lowline** (1963) - MOST POPULAR MODEL

**Designer:** Ken Woolley

**The Appeal:**
Single-story design perfect for families wanting modern living without stairs.

**Design Features:**
- Flat or low-pitched roof (though some buyers added gabled roofs)
- Open-plan living/dining/kitchen
- Strong horizontal emphasis
- Full-height windows
- Indoor-outdoor flow to garden/deck
- Carport integrated into design
- Usually 3-4 bedrooms
- Compact but spacious-feeling

**Award:**
**Ken Woolley's Lowline B won the 1967 Australian Institute of Architects Project House Design Award** ($10,000-$13,000 category)

**Why Most Popular:**
- **Accessible** - No stairs for elderly or young families
- **Affordable** - Single-level construction simpler than Split
- **Flexible** - Worked on relatively flat or gently sloping sites
- **Modern** - Clean lines, open plan appealed to young buyers
- **Expandable** - Could add carport, deck, second story later

**Variations:**
Multiple Lowline models offered different configurations:
- Lowline A, B, C with varying bedroom/bathroom layouts
- Gallery variation with covered walkway
- Courtyard integration in some models

---

#### **The Courtyard House** (c.1965)

**Concept:**
House enclosed internal courtyard on two sides, brick walls on other two sides.

**Design Features:**
- All main rooms (living, kitchen, bedrooms) face courtyard
- Courtyard becomes outdoor living room
- Roof slopes toward courtyard, emphasizing relationship
- Privacy from street and neighbors
- Protected outdoor space

**Appeal:**
- **Privacy** - Ideal for urban/suburban blocks
- **Climate** - Protected courtyard usable more months
- **Light** - All rooms receive natural light from courtyard
- **Children** - Safe enclosed play space visible from house

---

#### **The Highline** (Late 1960s)

**The Alternative:**
Two-story design with vertical emphasis - essentially the opposite of Lowline.

**Design Features:**
- Steep rooflines
- Vertical elements and proportions
- Two full stories
- Ground floor: living, kitchen, dining
- Upper floor: bedrooms, bathrooms
- Often dramatic entrance stair
- Balconies and decks

**Market:**
- Families wanting separation of living/sleeping zones
- Blocks where height captured better views
- Buyers wanting more dramatic statement

---

#### **Other Models:**

**Gallery**
- Covered walkway/breezeway connecting wings
- Indoor-outdoor circulation
- Suited to larger blocks

**Gambrel**
- Distinctive barn-style roofline
- More traditional appeal while maintaining modern planning

**Pavilion**
- Single-room pavilion design
- Could be main house or addition
- Minimal, elegant

**Hillside**
- Variation on Split Level for extreme slopes
- Multiple terraces
- Dramatic siting

---

### The Modular System - The Secret to Success

**The Grid:**
All P&S designs based on a **3-foot module** (later standardized to **4-foot module**).

**What This Meant:**

1. **Standardization** - Components manufactured efficiently
2. **Customization** - Easy to add or remove modules
3. **Flexibility** - Rooms could be enlarged by adding modules
4. **Interchangeability** - Roof designs could swap between models
5. **Expansion** - Second story could be added to single-level homes
6. **Construction Speed** - Modular system enabled 8-week build to lock-up

**Ken Woolley's Explanation:**
"The large grid and its section set up a rhythm like a beat in music, around which a composition of infill elements is composed."

**The 12-Foot Structural Grid:**
Woolley's own house (and many P&S designs) used 12-foot square units that could be:
- Staggered in plan
- Offset by 1/3 along contours
- Combined to create larger spaces
- Subdivided for smaller rooms

---

### Materials: "Off-the-Shelf" Australian Products Used Brilliantly

**The Philosophy:**
P&S used standard Australian building materials but through thoughtful design elevated them to architecture.

**Standard Materials:**
- **Brick veneer construction** (not solid brick - economical)
- **Gyprock interior wall cladding** (standard plasterboard)
- **Monier concrete tiles** (ubiquitous roof tiles)
- **Stegbar windows** (mass-produced aluminum windows)
- **Standard lumber** for framing
- **Clinker bricks** for feature walls
- **Western Red Cedar** for boards and paneling

**The Genius:**
These everyday materials, through:
- Honest expression (not hiding construction)
- Careful detailing
- Thoughtful proportions
- Integration with landscape

...became beautiful architecture.

**Powerhouse Museum Assessment:**
<cite index="1-11">"Brick veneer construction, Gyprock interior wall cladding, Monier concrete tiles, Stegbar windows and other components typical of 1960s cottage building were successfully adapted to state of the art domestic architecture."</cite>

## 5. Materials & Construction: Sydney School Characteristics

### What Made It "Sydney School"?

The Sydney School (also nicknamed "Nuts and Berries" style) was a regionalist reaction against International Style modernism's white-box purity.

**Core Characteristics:**

#### **Site Response**
- Built on sloping bushland sites (Sydney's characteristic terrain)
- Houses follow slope through split-level planning
- Minimal site disturbance
- Integration with existing trees and rock outcrops
- Bush settings fundamental to design

#### **Materiality**

**Brick:**
- **Clinker brick** - Imperfect bricks with burnt/melted surfaces, creating texture
- **Painted brick** - White or earth-toned
- **Bagged brick** - Mortar smeared over brick for textured finish
- **Face brick** - Exposed, honest

**Timber:**
- **Stained or oiled timber** (not painted) - Shows natural grain
- **Western Red Cedar** - For boarding, paneling, decking
- **Oregon/Douglas Fir beams** - Structural beams left exposed
- **Hardwood posts** - Structural elements as design features

**Roofing:**
- **Dark tiled roofs** - Monier concrete tiles in charcoal/brown
- **Low gutter lines**
- **Raked (sloped) rooflines** parallel to site slope
- **Skillion roofs** (single-slope) common
- **Flat roofs** on some models (though less common)

**Other:**
- **Stone** - Sydney sandstone for retaining walls, feature walls
- **Concrete** - Sometimes left exposed (Brutalist influence)
- **Glass** - Large expanses, clerestory windows

#### **Structural Expression**

**Post-and-Beam Construction:**
- Structural system exposed, not hidden
- Timber or steel posts visible
- Beams left natural (oiled, not painted)
- Honest expression of how building stands up

**Exposed Rafters:**
- Roof structure visible from below
- Creates visual rhythm
- Shows craftsmanship

#### **Indoor-Outdoor Integration**

**Large Windows:**
- Full-height glazing
- Clerestory windows (high windows for light/ventilation)
- Timber-framed (not aluminum initially)
- Operable for cross-ventilation

**Decks and Verandahs:**
- Extend living space outdoors
- Timber decking
- Often multiple levels
- Protected by roof overhangs

**Courtyards:**
- Internal or semi-enclosed
- Bring landscape into house
- Private outdoor rooms

#### **Color Palette**

**Neutral, Natural Tones:**
- Dark tiles (charcoal, brown, terracotta)
- Natural timber (oiled cedar, Oregon)
- Earth-toned or white painted brick
- Sandstone (natural grey/tan)

**Creating Warmth:**
<cite index="21-1">Natural materials were exploited, with dark tiles, clinker or painted bricks and stained timbers creating a feeling of warmth in the houses.</cite>

#### **Spatial Strategies**

**Split-Level Planning:**
- Half-levels create distinct zones
- Reduces corridor space
- Follows site contours
- Creates visual interest

**Open Planning:**
- Living/dining/kitchen flow together
- Minimal internal walls
- Flexibility of use
- Spatial continuity

**Varied Ceiling Heights:**
- Changes in level accompanied by ceiling height changes
- Creates drama and definition

### Influences on Sydney School

**Frank Lloyd Wright (American, 1867-1959):**
- Organic architecture
- Integration with landscape
- Natural materials in unaltered state
- Horizontal emphasis
- "Truth to materials"

**Japanese Traditional Architecture:**
- Truthful expression of materials and construction
- Spatial fluidity and flexibility
- Post-and-beam visible structure
- Indoor-outdoor continuity
- Simplicity and restraint

**Scandinavian Modern (Especially Alvar Aalto):**
- Bagged and painted brick walls
- Terracotta tiles on steeply sloping roofs
- Dark stained exposed timber structures
- Humanism in modernism
- Natural materials

**British New Brutalism (Alison & Peter Smithson):**
- Honest expression of materials and structure
- Deliberately rough, unrefined finishes
- Exposed concrete
- Rejection of International Style's refinement
- "As found" aesthetic

---

### Philip Goad's Five Sydney School Philosophies

Architectural historian Philip Goad identified five discernible approaches within Sydney School, with some architects employing multiple:

1. **Organic Approach** (Wright-influenced)
   - Materials in unaltered state
   - Blended with natural colors/textures of bush
   - Integration with landscape paramount

2. **Japanese Influence**
   - Post-and-beam expression
   - Spatial fluidity
   - Craftsmanship and detail
   - Materiality as philosophy

3. **Scandinavian Modernism** (Aalto-influenced)
   - Painted/bagged brick
   - Steeply sloping roofs
   - Warm, humanist modernism
   - Natural materials with refinement

4. **Case Study House Influence** (California)
   - Modular construction
   - Indoor-outdoor living
   - Affordability through standardization
   - Post-war optimism

5. **Brutalism**
   - Off-form concrete
   - Unity of form/structure/function
   - Celebration of construction process
   - Tough, uncompromising aesthetic

**P&S's Approach:**
Primarily drew from **#1 (Organic)**, **#2 (Japanese)**, and **#3 (Scandinavian)**, with occasional **#4 (Case Study)** influence in the modular system. Less Brutalist than some Sydney School architects.

## 8. Project Homes Movement Context

### What Are "Project Homes"?

**Definition:**
Project homes (also called "spec homes" or "production homes") are:
- Pre-designed house plans available for purchase
- Built by building companies on client's land
- Standardized designs with customization options
- More affordable than custom architect-designed homes
- Faster to build than one-off designs

**Post-WWII Australian Context:**

**The Housing Crisis (1945-1960s):**
- Massive housing shortage after WWII
- Returning servicemen needed homes
- Growing families (baby boom)
- Suburban expansion
- Car ownership enabling commutes

**Typical Solutions:**
- **Small Homes Service** (Government program)

  - Basic, functional designs
- **Fibro cottages** - Cheap, quick, but often poor quality
- **Standard suburban homes** - Brick veneer, traditional styling
- **Self-built homes** - Often by tradesman owners

**The Problem:**
Most affordable housing options meant:
- Poor design quality
- No architect involvement
- Traditional, conservative styling
- Little consideration of climate, orientation, site
- Cookie-cutter subdivision appearance

## 10. The Decline & Revival

### Corporate Changes (1973)

**The Sale:**
In 1973, Pettit & Sevitt sold the business in a partial sell-out to:
- **Landall** - West Australian company
- **CHI** (partial ownership)

**Impact:**
- Brian Pettit and Ron Sevitt stayed on in their roles
- Retained less control over direction
- Corporate priorities shifted
- Design quality concerns emerged

### Ken Woolley's Departure (1975)

**The End of an Era:**
1975 marked the end of Ken Woolley's 15-year collaboration with P&S.

**Why This Mattered:**
- Woolley had designed virtually all popular models
- His design philosophy defined the brand
- Personal architect consultations were his idea
- Loss of creative visionary
- Harder to maintain design standards

### Market Challenges (Late 1970s)

**Economic Factors:**

1. **Rampant Inflation**
   - Construction costs skyrocketing
   - Materials prices volatile
   - Labor costs rising
   - Profit margins squeezed

2. **Scarcity of Land Releases**
   - Prime North Shore largely built out
   - New subdivisions farther from Sydney CBD
   - Less attractive to target market
   - Transport costs increasing

3. **Energy Crisis** (1973, 1979)
   - Oil shocks affected entire economy
   - Building industry hit hard
   - Consumer confidence dropped
   - Discretionary spending (like new homes) declined

4. **Corporate Debt**
   - Landall brought existing debts
   - P&S operations affected
   - Cash flow problems
   - Unable to weather downturn

### Liquidation (1978)

**The End:**
After 17 years of operation, Pettit & Sevitt entered **liquidation in 1978**.

**Reasons:**
- Accumulation of economic pressures
- Loss of creative direction post-Woolley
- Corporate mismanagement by new owners
- Market conditions hostile
- Inability to adapt quickly enough

**Industry Impact:**
- Widely reported in architecture and business press
- Seen as loss for Australian design
- Demonstrated difficulty of maintaining quality in spec housing
- End of an era

## 11. Key Dates Timeline

**1933** - Ken Woolley born (May 29)

**1955** - Woolley graduates University of Sydney with First Class Honours, University Medal, Byera Hadley Travelling Scholarship

**1955-1956** - Woolley travels USA, Europe, UK studying architecture

**1958** - Woolley & Dysart win Australia's Family Home Competition for low-cost house

**1961 (April)** - **Pettit & Sevitt established** by Brian Pettit and Ron Sevitt

**1961 (Late)** - Ken Woolley and Michael Dysart begin designing for P&S

**1962 (Early)** - **First Split Level built** at Clifton Gardens; second at St Ives

**1962** - **Woolley House, Mosman** completed and wins Wilkinson Award same year

**1963** - **First exhibition centre opens** at Carlingford (Pennant Hills Rd & North Rocks Rd)

**1963** - Fisher Library, University of Sydney completed (Woolley) - wins Sulman Medal and RIBA Bronze Medal

**1964** - **Major exhibition centre opens** at St Ives (Richmond Road)

**1964** - Ancher Mortlock Murray & Woolley partnership formed; Woolley continues P&S work alongside

**1964** - Architecture students tour St Ives display village (documented)

**1966** - **Expansion to Canberra begins**

**1967** - **Woolley's Lowline B wins RAIA Project House Design Award**

**1968** - Tour of houses in **Belrose exhibition centre** documented

**1968** - Woolley wins another RAIA Project House Design Award for P&S model

**1968** - The Penthouses, Rushcutters Bay (Woolley/P&S) wins Wilkinson Award

**1971** - "Don's Party" play debuts (David Williamson) - later filmed in P&S home

**1973** - **Company sold to Landall/CHI** - Pettit and Sevitt stay on

**1975** - **Ken Woolley's involvement with P&S ends** (15 years of collaboration)

**1976** - "Don's Party" film released (Bruce Beresford) - set in P&S home

**1978** - **Company enters liquidation** - end of original P&S

**1993** - Ken Woolley receives RAIA Gold Medal (Australia's highest architectural honor)

**2001** - Woolley House, Mosman listed on NSW State Heritage Register

**2006** - Harry Seidler dies (March 9)

**2010** - **Val Sevitt relaunches Pettit+Sevitt** with children Carol and Colin

**2010** - Ken Woolley revisits and updates Lowline design for 21st century

**2015** - **Ken Woolley dies** (November 25, age 82)

**2022** - Woolley House receives **National Award for Enduring Architecture** (60 years after construction)

**2025** - Piper Press book on P&S history in production (over 200 illustrations, previously unpublished plans)

**2026** - P&S homes now 45-65 years old, increasingly recognized as heritage-worthy mid-century architecture
